# Digital Forensics Investigator

## Role overview
A digital forensics investigator examines digital artefacts to help establish what happened on a device or system. Work may include acquisition, preservation, analysis, timeline creation, and clear reporting. Procedures depend on the legal and organizational context.

## Core skills
- Operating-system concepts, file systems, storage, and common artefact locations.
- Evidence integrity, documentation, timestamps, time zones, and chain-of-custody concepts.
- Hashing to check whether evidence copies have changed.
- Basic scripting and careful data handling.
- Forensic tools and methods, learned in a controlled lab.
- Writing objective reports that distinguish facts, interpretations, and unknowns.

## Beginner learning roadmap
1. Learn files, directories, permissions, processes, and file-system basics.
2. Understand hashes and why original evidence should be preserved.
3. Use a synthetic disk image or training dataset in a lab.
4. Build a timeline from provided artefacts and record how each conclusion was reached.
5. Practice writing a concise report with limitations.

## Beginner projects
- Calculate SHA-256 hashes for sample files and compare them after copying.
- Build a timeline from a supplied synthetic event log.
- Create a lab report describing artefacts found, methods used, and uncertainties.

## Certifications and credentials
Requirements differ across employers and jurisdictions. Compare official training programs and current job descriptions. Some roles may require specialized experience, legal-process knowledge, or background checks.

## Example entry-level job keywords
Digital Forensics Intern, DFIR Analyst, Incident Response Analyst, and eDiscovery Analyst. These titles can represent different responsibilities; read each description carefully.

## Handling evidence
Practice only with files and datasets you are authorized to use. Do not alter original evidence; preserve working copies and document procedures.
